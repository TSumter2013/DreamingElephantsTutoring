Your Toolkit

Every definition here is used on the test. Read it out loud before each sitting. Saying it is how it sticks.

1 · What a derivative IS

The derivative \(f'(a)\) is the slope of the tangent line to \(y=f(x)\) at \(x=a\). It tells you how fast \(f\) is changing right there.

\[ f'(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \]

Why this works: \(\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\) is rise over run between two points that are \(h\) apart. Shrink \(h\) to 0 and the two points merge into one tangent slope.

  • \(f'(3)\) is ONE number: the slope at \(x=3\). If they give it to you, plug it in.
  • Straight segment on a graph? Its derivative is its slope. It's 0 only when the segment is flat.
  • "Tangent parallel to a line" means set \(f'(x)\) equal to that line's slope.
  • See \(\frac{F(x+h)-F(x)}{h}\) with \(h\to0\)? The answer is just \(F'(x)\).

2 · The rules (and why they work)

RuleFormulaWhy
Constant\((c)'=0\)A flat line has slope 0
Power\((x^n)'=nx^{n-1}\)Works for fractions and negatives too
Constant multiple\((cf)'=cf'\)The number just rides along
Sum\((f\pm g)'=f'\pm g'\)Do each piece separately
Exponential\((e^x)'=e^x\)\(e^x\) is its own slope
Product\((fg)'=f'g+fg'\)Each piece changes while the other holds still
Quotient\(\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)'=\frac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}\)"Low d-High minus High d-Low, over Low squared"
Myth buster: \((fg)'\) is NOT \(f'g'\), and \(\left(\frac fg\right)'\) is NOT \(\frac{f'}{g'}\). Proof with \(f=g=x\): \((x\cdot x)'=(x^2)'=2x\), but \(f'g'=1\cdot1=1\). Busted.

The "co" trick: every "co" function (cos, cot, csc, the pink rows) gets a minus. Partners: tan ↔ cot, sec ↔ csc. Swap each function for its "co" partner and add a minus. So \(\tan' = \sec^2\) becomes \(\cot' = -\csc^2\).

  • Reciprocal: \(\sec x=\frac1{\cos x}\), \(\csc x=\frac1{\sin x}\)
  • Bonus: \(\tan x\cdot\cot x=1\), \(\sec^2x-1=\tan^2x\)

6 · Lines

  • Point-slope: \(y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\). A line needs a point and a slope.
  • Parallel lines have equal slopes. Perpendicular slopes multiply to \(-1\).
  • Slope from \(ax+by=c\): solve for \(y\). Example: \(24x+3y=16\Rightarrow y=-8x+\tfrac{16}{3}\), slope \(-8\).

7 · The Chain Rule

Use it when a function has something other than plain \(x\) inside it. \(\sin(3x)\), \((x^2+1)^5\), \(e^{2x}\) and \(\sqrt{x^2+4}\) are all chains. Each one is a function inside another function (a composition).

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\Big[f\big(g(x)\big)\Big] = f'\big(g(x)\big)\cdot g'(x) \qquad\text{or}\qquad \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx} \]

In words: derivative of the outside (leave the inside alone) × derivative of the inside.

Why it multiplies: think of gears. If gear A spins 3 times as fast as gear B, and B spins 2 times as fast as C, then A spins 3 × 2 = 6 times as fast as C. Rates of change chained together multiply.
OutsideChain version (u is the inside)
\(u^n\)\(n\,u^{n-1}\cdot u'\)
\(\sqrt{u}\)\(\frac{1}{2\sqrt u}\cdot u'\)
\(e^{u}\)\(e^{u}\cdot u'\)
\(\sin u\)\(\cos u\cdot u'\)
\(\cos u\)\(-\sin u\cdot u'\)
\(\tan u\)\(\sec^2 u\cdot u'\)
\(\cot u\)\(-\csc^2 u\cdot u'\)
\(\sec u\)\(\sec u\tan u\cdot u'\)
\(\csc u\)\(-\csc u\cot u\cdot u'\)
The #1 chain mistake: forgetting the \(\cdot u'\). \(\frac{d}{dx}\sin(3x)\) is \(3\cos(3x)\), not \(\cos(3x)\). Mistake #2: changing the inside. It's \(\cos(3x)\), never \(\cos(3)\).

8 · Double & Triple Chains: the Onion Method

A double or triple chain is just the chain rule used 2 or 3 times in a row. Peel one layer at a time from the outside in. Each layer gives you one factor. Multiply all the factors.

The Layer Chart (draw this on every double or triple chain):

LayerWhat it isIts derivative (inside stays the same)
Outside\((\ \ )^3\)\(3\sin^2(4x)\)
Middle\(\sin(\ \ )\)\(\cos(4x)\)
Inside\(4x\)\(4\)

Multiply the last column: \(y'=3\sin^2(4x)\cdot\cos(4x)\cdot4=12\sin^2(4x)\cos(4x)\).

Find the layers by asking "what would I do LAST if I plugged in a number?" For \(\sin^3(4x)\) with \(x=1\): first 4·1, then sin of that, then cube it. The LAST step (cubing) is the outside layer. Work backwards from there.
Notation trap: \(\sin^3(4x)\) means \([\sin(4x)]^3\). The power is the OUTSIDE layer even though it's written next to "sin".

9 · Higher-Order Derivatives

A second derivative is the derivative of the derivative. Keep going for the third, fourth and so on.

OrderNotationMotion meaning
1st\(f'(x),\ y',\ \frac{dy}{dx}\)velocity \(v(t)=s'(t)\)
2nd\(f''(x),\ y'',\ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\)acceleration \(a(t)=v'(t)=s''(t)\)
3rd\(f'''(x),\ y''',\ \frac{d^3y}{dx^3}\)jerk (change in acceleration)
nth\(f^{(n)}(x),\ \frac{d^ny}{dx^n}\)
  • Simplify before you differentiate again. Rewrite \(\frac1x\) as \(x^{-1}\) and combine terms. A messy f′ makes f″ twice as messy.
  • The sine cycle repeats every 4: \(\sin x\to\cos x\to-\sin x\to-\cos x\to\sin x\). For the 35th derivative, divide by 4: remainder 3, so it's the 3rd step, \(-\cos x\).
  • Chains stack up: each derivative of \(\sin(2x)\) pulls out another 2. So \(\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\sin(2x)=-4\sin(2x)\).
  • Polynomials run out: a degree-n polynomial has all zeros from the \((n+1)\)th derivative on.

10 · Implicit Differentiation

Use it when y isn't by itself, like \(x^2+y^2=25\) or \(x^3+y^3=6xy\). You don't solve for y first.

The big idea: y is secretly a function of x, so every y-term is a chain. When you differentiate anything with y in it, tag it with \(y'\) (or \(\frac{dy}{dx}\)). Examples: \(\frac{d}{dx}[y^2]=2y\,y'\), \(\frac{d}{dx}[\sin y]=\cos y\cdot y'\), and \(\frac{d}{dx}[x^2]=2x\) gets no tag.
1 · Differentiate BOTH sides. Tag every y-term with y′.
2 · Move every y′ term to the left. Everything else goes right.
3 · Factor out y′.
4 · Divide to get y′ alone.
Product-rule trap: \(xy\) is a product. \(\frac{d}{dx}[xy]=(1)y+x\,y'\), not just \(y'\). The same goes for \(x^2y\) and \(6xy\).

Round 5 · Implicit6 problems · ~15 min0/6›
How this round works4 steps every time: (1) differentiate both sides, tagging every y-term with y′. (2) y′ terms left, everything else right. (3) Factor out y′. (4) Divide.
E1
\(x^2-y^2=9\)
\(y^2\to2y\,y'\)
  1. \(2x-2y\,y'=0\)
    Tag the y-term. 9 becomes 0.
  2. \(-2y\,y'=-2x\)
    Move the 2x to the right.
  3. \(y'=\frac{x}{y}\)
    Divide by −2y. The negatives cancel.
\(y'=\frac{x}{y}\)
E2
\(xy=6\)
xy is a product. Use the product rule.
  1. \((1)y+x\,y'=0\)
    Product rule: derivative of x is 1, derivative of y is y′.
  2. \(x\,y'=-y\)
    Move y to the right.
  3. \(y'=-\frac{y}{x}\)
    Divide by x.
\(y'=-\frac{y}{x}\)
Trap\(\frac{d}{dx}[xy]\) is NOT \(y'\). It's \(y+xy'\).
E3
\(y^2+3xy=x^3\)
3xy needs the product rule.
  1. \(2y\,y'+3y+3x\,y'=3x^2\)
    Tag \(y^2\). Product rule on 3xy.
  2. \(2y\,y'+3x\,y'=3x^2-3y\)
    y′ terms stay left. Move 3y to the right.
  3. \(y'(2y+3x)=3x^2-3y\)
    Factor out y′.
  4. \(y'=\frac{3x^2-3y}{2y+3x}\)
    Divide.
\(y'=\frac{3x^2-3y}{2y+3x}\)
E4
\(\cos y+y=x^2\)
\(\cos y\to-\sin y\cdot y'\)
  1. \(-\sin y\cdot y'+y'=2x\)
    Both y-terms get tagged.
  2. \(y'(1-\sin y)=2x\)
    Factor out y′. Put the 1 first, it reads cleaner.
  3. \(y'=\frac{2x}{1-\sin y}\)
    Divide.
\(y'=\frac{2x}{1-\sin y}\)
E5

Find y′, then plug in x = 2 AND y = 3.
  1. \(2x+2y\,y'=0\Rightarrow y'=-\frac{x}{y}\)
    Same steps as the circle in I1.
  2. At \((2,3)\): \(y'=-\frac23\)
    Plug in both coordinates.
  3. \(y-3=-\frac23(x-2)\)

\(x^2+4y^2=4\). Find \(y''\). BOSS

  5. \(4y^2+x^2=4\), so \(y''=-\frac{4}{16y^3}\)

Test Day: 5 habits

Your teacher grades the process, not just the answer. These five habits turn partial credit into full credit.

1

Rewrite line first

Before any derivative, write "Rewrite?" and simplify roots, fractions and cancels, or write "none".

2

Letters first, numbers last

On given-value and graph problems, write the rule in f, g, f′, g′ before plugging in.

3

Parentheses everywhere

Every box goes into the formula inside ( ). Touch each box as you copy it.

4

Write fraction exponents out

"7/3 − 1 = 7/3 − 3/3 = 4/3." Never in your head.

5

Answer what they asked

Circle the target (an x-value, an equation, a number). Check that your last line matches it.
